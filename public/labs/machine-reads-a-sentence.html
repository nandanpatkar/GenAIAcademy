Act 1 · Chapter 1

A computer has never seen a word

To a processor, the letters C-A-T are just shapes. It has no idea a cat is furry, small, or a pet. Silicon understands exactly one thing: numbers. So before any "intelligence" can happen, every word must become a number — that's the very first step of the pipeline above.

Act 1 · Chapter 2

Step one: chop it into tokens

This is the single most important idea in the whole course. LLMs do not read words. Not sentences. Not even characters. They read tokens — the "language atoms" a model actually receives. Everything downstream (context windows, pricing, embeddings, RAG, hallucinations, speed) starts here.

Question 1

Why not just store whole words?

Because words are impossible to store. Just for the verb run you'd need run, running, runs, runner, runners, ran… now multiply by Hindi, Chinese, Japanese, Arabic, emojis, code, URLs, file paths. The vocabulary explodes into billions. Instead the tokenizer keeps ~100k–200k reusable pieces that recombine into millions of words.

internationalization → inter nation al ization
Question 2

Why do tokens include spaces, and why does "OpenAI" split?

The tokenizer doesn't split on spaces — it splits into statistically common pieces. " language" (with a leading space) is stored as one token because it appears so often, which saves a token every time. And "OpenAI" isn't in the vocabulary, but Open and AI already are — so it reuses them. Punctuation carries meaning, so . and , get their own tokens too.

OpenAI's → Open AI 's
Question 3

How does it decide the splits? (BPE)

Modern models use Byte Pair Encoding. During training it starts from raw characters, counts which neighbouring pair appears together most often, merges that pair into a new token, and repeats millions of times. Common sequences fuse into single tokens; rare ones stay in pieces.

t · h · e → merge top pair → th · e → again → the
Question 4

Characters vs tokens — and the ~4× rule

A rough rule is 1 token ≈ 4 characters in English — but it's only an average. Type aaaaaaaa… in the box above and the ratio collapses: the tokenizer has never seen that run, so it burns far more tokens. Code, emojis, and other languages all have different averages.

The Chars / token stat in the playground updates live so you can feel this yourself.

Question 5

Why AI engineers obsess over token count

💵
Pricing
You pay per token — 1000 in + 500 out = 1500 billed. Not words.
📏
Context window
"400K context" means 400,000 tokens (~300K words), not words.
📚
RAG budget
Docs 90K + history 20K + system 10K = 120K → only 8K left to answer.
⚡
Latency
Fewer input tokens → faster inference. Concise prompts win twice.

This is why enterprise RAG systems aggressively chunk, compress, summarize, and rerank — every token spent on context is a token stolen from the answer. Once you think in token budgets, embeddings, attention and inference all click into place.

Act 1 · Chapter 3

Every token gets an address, not a meaning

The tokenizer keeps a giant dictionary. Each token maps to a fixed number — its Token ID. But here's the trap beginners fall into: the ID carries no meaning. It's just a row number, like an employee badge or a passport number.

Cat1
Dog2
Tiger3
Lion4

The problem: Is Cat (1) closer to Dog (2) than to Tiger (3)? The numbers say "a little," but that's an accident of counting. 1, 2, 3, 4 hold zero relationship. We need something far richer.

Act 1 · Chapter 4

The big idea: describe by properties

Instead of one number per animal, what if we describe each one with several numbers — legs, fur, wild-or-pet, size? Suddenly the numbers mean something. Look what happens to Cat and Dog.

Cat = [4,1,0,1,2] and Dog = [4,1,0,1,3] — almost identical. Tiger and Lion cluster together too. The rows are now vectors, and similar animals have similar vectors. This is exactly what an embedding is — just with 1536 properties instead of 5.

Act 1 · Chapter 5

But nobody writes those properties

Here's the magic. No engineer sits down and labels "dimension 1 = legs, dimension 2 = fur." Instead the model starts with pure random noise and slowly discovers useful properties on its own, by reading billions of sentences. Press the button to watch random numbers organize themselves.

Apple  ·  Banana

Why does Dog land next to Cat? Because they appear in near-identical sentences — "the ___ drinks", "cute ___", "pet ___". Every time the model guesses wrong, backpropagation nudges those numbers a hair. Billions of nudges later, meaning emerges. This is called distributional semantics.

Act 1 · Chapter 5½

So what is a vector, really?

You've been building them without the name. A vector is just an ordered list of numbers — and every number is a coordinate that places the word somewhere in space. Cat = [4,1,0,1,2] isn't a definition of "cat"; it's an address. Words with nearby addresses have nearby meanings.

In the 2D map above, Cat and Dog sit close while Tiger and Lion cluster elsewhere. That distance is the meaning. We can't draw 1536 dimensions, but the math is identical — measure the gap between two vectors and you've measured how related two words are. Later chapters (cosine similarity, semantic search, RAG) are all built on exactly this idea.

A token is a piece of text. A token ID is its address in the dictionary. A vector is the actual list of numbers stored at that address — and that's what enters the transformer. Everything downstream is just math on these vectors.

Act 2 · Chapter 6

The embedding matrix: one giant lookup table

Now scale the idea up. The model stores a huge table: one row per token (about 150,000 of them) and one column per learned property (often 1536). A Token ID is just the row number you look up. Click any token to pull its vector.

Click a token →

Each square is one dimension. Warm = high, cool = low.

This table is 150,000 × 1536 ≈ 230 million numbers — and that's just the input layer. The Token ID is the address. The vector is what actually lives there.

Act 2 · Chapter 7

Why 1536 dimensions?

Describe a person with 2 numbers — you can't say much. With 100, more. With 1536, you can encode astonishingly rich relationships without concepts bleeding into each other. So why not a million? Because every transformer layer must multiply these vectors — more dimensions means more memory, more compute, slower answers. 1536 is a balance of richness and cost.

Act 2 · Chapter 8

Self-attention: words look at each other

A word alone is ambiguous. To understand "Agents", the model lets every word look at every other word and decide which ones matter. Click a word below to see where its attention flows — brighter links mean stronger attention.

click a word to see what it pays attention to

When you click "Agents", notice how it leans on "AI" — together they mean something specific. That's attention deciding which context is relevant. This is the single most important operation in a transformer.

Act 2 · Chapter 9

The same word, a different vector

Before attention, "Apple" has one fixed embedding. After attention reads the sentence around it, that vector changes — pulling toward "fruit" or toward "company." Same token, different meaning, because context rewrote its numbers. Flip the sentence and watch the vector shift.

Contextual vector for "Apple" — meaning: fruit

This is why transformers feel intelligent: the meaning of a word is computed fresh, in context, every single time.

Act 3 · Chapter 10

Picking the next token

After all the layers, the final vector is scored against every word in the vocabulary. Those raw scores become probabilities through softmax. Temperature controls the mood: low means safe and predictable, high means creative and wild. Drag the slider.

Prompt: "The cat drinks ___". At low temperature the model almost always says milk. Crank it up and rarer words get a real chance. Same numbers, different creativity.
